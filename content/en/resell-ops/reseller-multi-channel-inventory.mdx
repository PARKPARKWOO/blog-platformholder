You list one pair of shoes on two channels. Only one of them needs to sell, and twice the people see it. Obvious call.

Then you find out about the sale roughly thirty minutes late. You rush to pull the other listing down — and there is already an order sitting on it.

From here you have no options. One side gets cancelled, and that side keeps the penalty and the dent in your seller rating. Next month the same thing happens again.

<Callout type="note" title="Summary">
- Double selling is not a counting problem. It is a **missing column for promises**
- Physical quantity and **sellable quantity** are different numbers
- There is always a gap between when you delist and when the channel reflects it
- Single-unit unique items are safest on one channel only
- Log cancellations with a reason, or the pattern stays invisible
</Callout>

## 1. The problem is not the quantity, it's the promise

Open the stock sheet and the quantity reads exactly 1. You have never miscounted.

What is wrong is not the counting — it is **how many promises that 1 is carrying**. The moment it goes up on two channels, that single pair carries two sales promises simultaneously. Both cannot be honored.

A spreadsheet's quantity cell answers only "how many are there right now." **There was never a cell for "what is this unit already promised to."** So the sheet stays silent until the last possible moment. The number is still 1, after all.

## 2. Delisting time and reflected time are not the same

The second failure point is lag.

You see the sale notification, open the app, find the other listing, take it down. That costs a few minutes. The channel then takes its own time to reflect that removal in listings. Cached lists and search results can hold on a little longer still.

**This gap does not add up with each channel — it multiplies.** With three channels, one confirmed sale means two places to pull down, and one of them being slow is enough. Pulling listings by hand without a fixed order means a different channel is late every time.

If the sale lands overnight, the gap stretches until morning. That is why double sales cluster in the small hours.

## 3. Stock you don't actually have leaks into sellable quantity

The third failure point is that **having it physically does not mean you can sell it**.

Units can be sitting in the room and still be off-limits.

- Sold and confirmed, but not yet shipped
- Already shipped and in transit (still on the sheet because of refusal or return risk)
- Returned and under inspection — resale eligibility not yet decided
- On its way to a channel warehouse, or waiting for intake inspection
- Purchased but not yet delivered to you

All five stay in physical quantity but **none of them is sellable quantity**. The last one is the most dangerous: listing something because it is "arriving soon" leaves you holding only the sales promise if the purchase falls through or intake is rejected.

## 4. A cancellation costs more than the penalty fee

Most sellers already know points 1 through 3. Unique items keep going up on multiple channels anyway, because the cost of a cancellation gets counted as the penalty fee and nothing else.

In practice these come attached:

- Channels differ, but cancellation and non-fulfillment carry sanctions, and repeats affect exposure or selling privileges
- It lands on your seller rating and reviews, which take time to work back up
- The cancelled item has to be relisted — photos, description, price, all reassembled
- Buyer communication costs time, and it rarely ends with an apology
- The sale date for that item slides back entirely

**The dividing line is whether you have ever put the gain from doubled exposure and the cost of one cancellation in the same table.** For fast-moving, low-priced items exposure usually wins. For high-priced, single-unit items the cancellation cost usually wins.

## 5. Which is why one quantity cell cannot answer

Put together, the questions a stock sheet has to answer are not "how many."

- How many can I **safely sell right now**
- **Which channels** is that quantity committed to
- When it sells, **which listing do I pull first**

One quantity cell answers none of the three. That is not a reason to abandon the sheet — a few extra columns cover most of it.

## What you can change in the sheet today

<Steps>
<Step title="Add a sellable quantity column">
Next to physical quantity, add a separate column computed as `sellable = physical − sold/unshipped − in transit − in return inspection − moving to warehouse − purchased but undelivered`. Make every listing and delisting decision **from this column only**. Physical quantity is for stock counts.
</Step>
<Step title="Mark each item exclusive or shared">
`Exclusive` means one channel only; `shared` means listed on several. One character at item creation is enough. **Default anything with sellable quantity of 1 to exclusive.** When you make an exception, the only check is whether you could absorb a cancellation at that price.
</Step>
<Step title="Fix the delisting order in writing">
Decide in advance which channel comes down first when something sells. One criterion: **whichever is slowest to reflect changes, or has the most eyes on it, goes first.** A fixed order removes judgment from the panicked moment, and stops a different channel being the late one each time.
</Step>
<Step title="Log listings and delistings as rows">
Record when an item went up on which channel and when it came down — in a separate sheet, one row each, not squeezed into the item row. It looks like overhead until the day you need to answer "I definitely pulled it, so why did an order arrive."
</Step>
<Step title="Record cancellations with a reason code">
`double-sale`, `stock-mismatch`, `price-error`, `shipping-delay` is enough of a vocabulary. Three fields per incident: date, channel, reason. After two or three months you can see which channel combinations and which price bands produce accidents. Without the log, every incident just reads as bad luck.
</Step>
</Steps>

The first two alone remove most unique-item accidents. The other three exist so you can find out *why* the remaining ones happened.

## When multi-channel listing is still the right call

**If quantity is comfortable and turnover is fast, list on several channels.** This post is not an argument for fewer channels.

The rule is simple. If sellable quantity is 2 or more and one sale still leaves stock for the next order, sharing costs you little. If sellable quantity is 1 with no replacement, the price of doubled exposure is the entire cancellation risk.

<Callout type="warning" title="On channel policies">
Rules for listing the same item across channels, and the sanctions for cancellation or non-fulfillment, differ by channel and change often. This post is a general operational view, not an interpretation of any channel's terms. Verify the current terms and seller policy of the channels you actually use.
</Callout>

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it fine to list the same item on several channels at once?**
For items with spare quantity and fast turnover, extra exposure usually wins. The problem is the unique, single-unit item. The moment one physical unit carries two sales promises, one of them has to be cancelled. The higher the price and the less replaceable the item, the more a single-channel listing costs you less.

**Can a spreadsheet prevent reseller double selling and overselling?**
Not completely, but most of it. The key move is adding a separate "sellable quantity" column next to physical quantity and making every list/delist decision from that column alone. Add a per-item rule for whether it is exclusive to one channel or shared across several, and the remaining risk narrows down to a handful of unique items.

**How do I calculate sellable quantity?**
Take physical quantity and subtract what is already promised: sold but not yet shipped, in transit, under return inspection, and moving to or awaiting intake at a channel warehouse. These units are still visible in the building or in the system, so they stay in physical quantity — but they are not available to sell again right now.

**How should I track stock I sent ahead to a channel warehouse?**
Count it as sellable from the moment the channel confirms intake, not from the moment you shipped it. If you count units in transit as sellable, they get listed elsewhere, and a single failed intake inspection removes the whole quantity while the sales promise remains. Record a warehouse move as a status change, not as a stock movement.

**What should I look for in a reseller inventory tool?**
Three things before feature count: does it separate sellable quantity from physical quantity, does it keep listing, delisting, and cancellation as history, and where does it store your channel account credentials. The last one is harder to reverse than switching tools, so check first whether it asks you to upload passwords, cookies, or sessions to its servers.
