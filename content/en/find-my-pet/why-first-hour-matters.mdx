The leash slips during a walk, or you take your eyes off them for a second.

Most owners experience the same sequence: a minute when the heart stops, then thirty minutes when the head goes blank. It's hard to remember what to do.

But that exact half hour is what most decides whether you find them again.

## Why the first one to two hours decide the most

Because it's the window when the animal is **most likely to still be close by**.

Pets unfamiliar with the outdoors rarely sprint off. They instinctively tuck into something tight and dark and wait to see what happens — under parked cars, narrow alleys, beneath stairs, inside planters, utility nooks.

That's why, in this window, covering the immediate area thoroughly beats widening the radius. It's also why owner-found reunions cluster here.

As time passes the situation changes character entirely. The animal leaves its hiding spot and starts moving toward something familiar, and from that point you need **many neighbors' eyes** more than your own.

<Callout type="important" title="What the first hour actually is">
The first one to two hours are **the time you find them yourself**. Everything after is **the time other people find them for you**.
Which is why the searching and the spreading have to start together. If you only start telling people once your own search runs out, the information arrives too late to be seen when it's needed.
</Callout>

## What changes as hours pass

### 1. The range widens

Once they've confirmed the first hiding spot is safe, animals start moving toward familiar landmarks. How far they get varies so much with terrain, roads, weather, and temperament that a single number would be misleading.

The **reachable-area map** on a Find-My-Pet missing post handles this as an explicit estimate. From elapsed time and species it draws three bands — Core, Likely, Possible — using the actual walking road network for dogs and a circular estimate for cats and other animals.

It exists to help you **decide where to look next**. It is not a boundary, and it should never be used to rule an area out.

### 2. Posts you already made sink

Anything you put into group chats, neighborhood cafes, or social feeds slides down as newer content arrives. Almost nobody discovers yesterday's post today.

So it helps to keep the information at **one fixed link** and re-surface that link across channels, rather than rewriting the post everywhere. Keep one page current and every channel stays current with it.

### 3. Your own stamina and judgment drop

Searching through the night is unsustainable. A sharp first hour, plus more people called in, beats two more hours of exhausted wandering.

## Three principles for using the first hour well

### 1. Run things in parallel

Searching and notifying are not sequential.

With two people, **one walks and one files and shares**. With three, two head in different directions and one plays dispatcher. Three different paths, once each, covers far more than one path three times.

If you're alone, publish the post first. You can do it while walking, and people start seeing it while you're still out.

### 3. Stay calm

Loud noises and running steps push an already-frightened animal further away.

The familiar rustle of a treat bag, a gentle voice calling their name, or the squeak of a known toy works much better. For cats, cut the noise entirely and re-check the same spots during quiet pre-dawn hours.

## What you don't need to do in the first hour

When panicked we want to do *more*. But these four barely change if you start one to three hours later:

- **Printing flyers** — later is fine. And since a flyer's QR code points at your post, filing the post comes first anyway
- **Deciding a reward amount** — leaving it blank or marking it negotiable still gets tips
- **Calling insurers or registries** — do it calmly after 24 hours
- **Running social ads** — they rarely reach the people actually walking that neighborhood

The first hour doesn't come back. Spend it on two things only: walking, and telling people.
