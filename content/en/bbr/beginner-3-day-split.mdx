There's a specific moment that catches almost every gym newcomer off-guard.

**You're standing in front of a machine and have no idea how to adjust it.** "Do I train chest first or back?" Nothing feels obvious. And a week later, back at the gym: "Wait — how much did I lift last time?" You can't remember.

Most people who quit the gym in their first month don't quit because of willpower. They quit because there are too many unresolved decisions standing between them and the workout.

This is the blueprint for surviving the first four weeks. You can follow it on one sheet of paper — no app required.

## Three reasons beginners quit in month one

Quitting is rarely about fitness. It's **three specific points of friction that repeat every session.**

**First: you don't know how to set up the equipment.**

How high should the bench seat go? Where do you pin the weight on a cable machine? These questions are genuinely hard to answer as a beginner, and asking a staff member every single time feels awkward. A bad setup doesn't just hurt efficiency — for beginners, it can also raise the risk of injury.

**Second: there's no framework for what to do or in what order.**

Search for workout routines online and you'll find a different answer on every channel. At the gym, this usually means "whatever machine is free" — which leads to hitting the same muscles too often or leaving others completely untrained.

**Third: without records, there's no progress to track.**

To know whether to add weight or reps next week, you need last week's numbers. Without them, every session starts from zero.

<Callout type="note" title="Why a 3-day split works well for beginners">
  For most beginners, a 3-day split — training chest, back, and legs on separate days — is a commonly recommended starting point. Each muscle group gets at least 48 hours of recovery, and training one area per session makes it easier to focus on learning the movements and equipment. That said, how your body responds varies from person to person, so adjust based on how you're recovering.
</Callout>

## The first four weeks — five steps

<Steps>
  <Step title="Step 1 — Put chest, back, and legs on separate days">
    The core of a 3-day split isn't the exercise list — it's **the spacing.**

    Leave at least 48 hours before repeating a muscle group. Mon-Wed-Fri or Tue-Thu-Sat, with one rest day between sessions, is the default shape.

    | Session | Focus | Example exercises |
    |---|---|---|
    | Day 1 | Chest | Bench press, incline dumbbell press, cable fly |
    | Day 2 | Back | Lat pulldown, seated row, one-arm dumbbell row |
    | Day 3 | Legs | Leg press, leg extension, leg curl |

    If the schedule slips, that's fine. **Keep "three sessions this week"** and go in order. Back the day after chest is fine; chest the day after chest is not.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 2 — Fill each session with 3–4 exercises, compounds first">
    Order matters: **big compound movements first.**

    - Front: 1–2 compounds (bench press, lat pulldown, leg press)
    - Back: 2 single-joint accessories (cable fly, leg extension, leg curl)

    Attempting a heavy compound while already fatigued means form breaks before the muscle does. Putting the hard movement where your energy is lowers injury risk.

    In a 45–60 minute session, 3–4 exercises per muscle group at 3 sets each is a reasonable starting shape.

    <Callout type="tip" title="Keep a backup routine for home">
      Save one bodyweight routine for weeks you can't make it to the gym. Push-ups, squats, and planks are enough. A backup turns "this week is ruined" into "one session at home."
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 3 — Check the setup points on the machines you'll use">
    There's one reference for machine setup: **does your joint line up with the machine's pivot?**

    Judge these four against that reference and most setup mistakes disappear.

    - **Seat height** — on leg extension, your knee should sit at the pad's pivot; on chest press, the handles should meet mid-chest
    - **Grip width** — lat pulldown slightly wider than shoulders; bench press wide enough that elbows land near 90° at the bottom
    - **Weight pin** — a load you can move for 10 reps, with one lighter warm-up set first
    - **Safety bar height** — for anything pressed over your body, set it so a failed rep won't pin your chest

    Checking two or three machines the night before your session removes the freeze-up in front of an unfamiliar one.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 4 — Log load, sets, and reps every session">
    Three columns are all you need: **load · sets · reps.**

    - Write it **immediately** after finishing the exercise. Numbers reconstructed at home are guesses
    - If reps differ across sets, write them as they were (e.g., 60kg 8/7/6)
    - Mark any set where something hurt or your form wobbled

    That table is the input to next week's plan. After four weeks, your progress curve becomes visible.

    <Callout type="warning" title="No log, no next week">
      Progressive overload is a function of last week's numbers. Without a record you can't tell whether you progressed, so the same weight repeats indefinitely.
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 5 — Adjust loads after four weeks using the log">
    At the four-week mark, each exercise falls into one of three buckets.

    - **Hit every target rep across all sets** → add 2.5–5kg
    - **Fell short on the last set** → hold the same load one more week
    - **Something hurt** → swap that exercise for a similar one, or cut its volume

    The first four weeks are for **building form and the logging habit**, not for adding plates. Fill them properly and the next step becomes a calculation.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Wrap-up

Quitting the gym in month one isn't about willpower. It's about **the unfamiliarity of standing in front of a machine you don't know how to use, the anxiety of having no routine, and the loop of starting from scratch every time you walk in**.

Write the 3-day split down. Check four setup points before you go. Log three columns every session. A small structure like that is what makes the difference between month one and month four.

Your first workout is the hardest. After that, what you wrote down does the guiding.
