Sunday, 9 PM.

You open the planner, telling yourself "this week I'll start again." An hour later, **you still haven't decided what to do tomorrow.**

You give up and write "same as before." Monday at the gym, you do the same three sets you've done for months.

This pattern eats whole quarters, not weeks.

## It's not willpower — it's decision fatigue

Planning a week of training is not one decision. It is **at least seven decisions bundled together**, which is exactly why it takes an hour and why it keeps getting postponed.

Your brain has to handle all of these at once:

- **Goal** — fat loss / strength / endurance, in what priority
- **Split** — 2-day, 3-day, or 5-day, depending on availability
- **Muscle grouping** — which muscles go together each session
- **Exercise selection** — dozens of options per muscle group
- **Sets · reps · load** — how to progress from last week
- **Recovery** — how many days between same muscle hits
- **Time** — what fits in 30 / 50 / 90 minutes

Doing this every week is **decision fatigue**. By Sunday evening — after a full work week of choices — you have nothing left for it.

<Callout type="note" title="What decision fatigue actually does">
The popular "willpower is a fuel tank that runs dry" model is contested — large multi-lab replications have not reliably reproduced it, so treat it as a metaphor rather than a settled finding. What is easier to observe in your own week is the behavior: when a decision feels heavy, people tend to fall back on **the safest default** — usually whatever they did last time. That's why sitting down to plan still produces the same routine, plateaus arrive, and weeks get skipped.
</Callout>

## The first fix: reverse the order

**Six of those seven decisions fall out of one of them.** The first thing to settle isn't "which exercises" — it's **how many sessions you can realistically make this month, and how many minutes each one gets.**

Lock those two numbers and the rest narrows on its own.

| Sessions per week | Split that follows | Volume per session |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Full body | 4–5 compound lifts in 45–60 min |
| 3 | 3-day split (chest / back / legs) | 3–4 exercises per muscle group |
| 4 | Upper-lower, twice through | 3 main + 1 accessory in 45 min |
| 5+ | Further split (+ dedicated shoulder/arm day) | 30–45 min blocks |

Starting from "which days am I free" drags scheduling variables into the plan and multiplies the decisions again. **Lock the count, not the calendar** — that's the practical trick that makes planning light.

## "Just hire a trainer" — why it doesn't work for everyone

Offline PT is a proven solution, but paying for it purely to get a plan is an expensive way to solve that half of the problem.

- **Time cost** — coordinating with the trainer's schedule
- **Money cost** — in Korea, commonly around ₩50,000–100,000 per session × twice a week, so roughly ₩400,000–800,000/month. Rates vary widely by city, gym, and trainer, and other markets differ
- **Space cost** — must travel to where the trainer is
- **Recurrence** — the package ends and you face the decision loop again

**Form coaching and planning are different problems.** Form correction and live feedback are overwhelmingly better in person. "What do I do this week, for how many sets" is a calculation from conditions. For most working gym-goers, solving only the second half already removes most of the weekly weight.

## Logging removes one more decision

Progressive overload isn't intuition — it's **a function of last week's numbers.** Which is why people who log don't have to decide what load to use.

Finished bench press at 60kg × 8 last week? The next step is usually one of two options:

- Same load, one more rep (60kg × 9)
- Fewer reps, more load (62.5kg × 7)

Without a record, that calculation is impossible and you start from scratch every week. **The cheapest investment in lighter planning is a log.** Three columns — load, sets, reps — on paper or in a notes app is enough.

## The real decision-killer — three rules written down in advance

Instead of judging fresh every week, **settle the criteria once** and afterwards you only read the log and execute. Write these three sentences at the top of your plan. They permanently remove three or four of the seven decisions.

**Rule 1 — When load goes up**

"Hit the target reps on **every set** → add 2.5–5kg next session."

This is commonly called double progression. Reps climb first; when they max out, load climbs and reps reset to the bottom of the range. With an 8–12 target, you push to 12 across all sets, add weight, and start building from 8 again. The question "what weight should I use this week" disappears at that point.

**Rule 2 — How you restart after missing**

"One week missed → resume the same plan at 10% less load. Two weeks or more → first week at half the exercises."

Coming back is hard because "where do I restart from" is a brand-new decision. Decide it in advance and a missed week becomes a branch inside the plan rather than the end of it. Delete "cram the missed sessions into next week" from the option list entirely.

**Rule 3 — When a painful exercise gets swapped**

"Pain in the same exercise two sessions in a row → swap it for another movement in the same area and watch for two weeks."

Re-deciding "should I push through this?" every time usually ends with pushing through. A numeric swap condition automates the judgment. If pain is severe or persists in daily life, of course, a professional comes before the rule.

<Callout type="tip" title="Rules belong at the top of the plan">
  A rule that lives only in your head doesn't fire under decision fatigue. Written on the first line of the plan or notes app, Sunday night collapses to a single judgment: how many sessions can I make this week.
</Callout>

## The 10-minute Sunday checklist

Once the three rules are in place, weekly planning is five questions. Copy them down and just fill in the answers.

1. **How many sessions did I actually do last week?** — the real number, not the planned one
2. **What's realistic this week?** — pick within ±1 of that real number
3. **How many minutes per session?** — rounded to 15-minute blocks
4. **Any exercise that triggered Rule 1?** — add load only where every set hit the target reps
5. **Any pain or missed sessions?** — if yes, apply Rules 2 and 3; if no, copy last week's plan as-is

Only questions 2 and 3 involve a fresh judgment; the rest is transcription from the log. That's where planning drops from an hour to ten minutes.

## Closing — "A sustainable pace"

The reason most weeks slip isn't time and isn't willpower. It's **the weight of deciding how to fill the week.**

Reducing that weight is a matter of order, not tooling. Lock the count and the minutes, keep a log, and let the rest follow. Once that holds, Sunday nights get lighter.
