If you've ever spent Sunday night thinking "what should I train this week?" — and then defaulted to the same routine, or skipped the week entirely — you're not alone.

Planning is hard not because information is missing but because **too many things need deciding at once.** Most of them, though, fall out of two numbers you already know.

This guide is the order that gets a week planned on one sheet of paper. Fifteen minutes is enough.

## Planning feels heavy because the order is backwards

Most people start with **"which exercises should I do?"** That opens dozens of options at once, which is why an hour passes with nothing written down.

Flip the order and the options collapse:

1. How many sessions can I make → the split is decided
2. How long is each session → the number of exercises is decided
3. Only then, pick the exercises

The first two are facts about your schedule, not judgment calls. The third narrows to a handful of candidates once the first two are locked.

<Callout type="tip" title="Why counts beat weekdays">
  Fix weekdays and one late night at work turns into "the plan failed." Set "three sessions this week" and Tuesday/Thursday/Saturday counts just as much as Wednesday/Friday/Sunday. Goals that are easy to judge are the ones that survive.
</Callout>

## Five steps, fifteen minutes

<Steps>
  <Step title="Step 1 — Lock the number of sessions first">
    Use **the last four weeks of reality**, not this week's motivation.

    Count how many times you actually trained over the past four weeks, take the weekly average, and add **exactly one**. If last month averaged two, this week is three.

    No history to look at? Start at three. It leaves recovery room while still being frequent enough to build a habit.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 2 — Set session length in 15-minute blocks">
    Count only **actual training time** — no commute, no changing, no shower.

    Round to 30 / 45 / 60 / 90. A vague "about an hour and a half" isn't a plan, it's a hope, and it makes session volume impossible to calculate.

    <Callout type="note">
      45–60 minutes covers most goals. On days that come up short, cut the number of exercises instead of abandoning the session.
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 3 — Assign the split that matches your count">
    The split is a **result** of Step 1, not a preference.

    | Sessions/week | Split | Session shape |
    |---|---|---|
    | 2 | Full body | 2–3 upper + 2 lower |
    | 3 | 3-day (chest / back / legs) | 3–4 per muscle group |
    | 4 | Upper-lower, twice | 3 main + 1 accessory |
    | 5+ | Further split (+ shoulder/arm day) | 30–45 min blocks |

    Leaving at least 48 hours before a muscle group repeats is the common recommendation. That spacing is exactly why a 3-day split is the standard suggestion for beginners.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 4 — Fill each session starting with compound lifts">
    Order matters: **big compound movements first.**

    - Front: 1–2 compounds (bench press, lat pulldown, squat, leg press)
    - Back: 2 single-joint accessories (cable fly, leg extension, leg curl)

    Attempting a heavy compound while already fatigued means form breaks before the muscle does. Putting the hard movement where your energy is lowers injury risk.

    <Callout type="tip">
      If exercise names don't come to mind, choose the machines you'll use first and fill in what each one does. It also cuts the time you spend wandering the gym floor.
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 5 — Pick starting loads and draw the log columns">
    Start at a load you can move for **10 reps with clean form.**

    The last rep or two should be hard while your position still holds. The first week builds form and the logging habit — not your one-rep max.

    Then draw three columns next to the plan: **load · sets · reps.** Those three columns are the input to next week's plan.

    <Callout type="warning" title="No log, no next week">
      Progressive overload is a function of last week's numbers. Finish 60kg × 8 and the choices narrow to 60kg × 9 or 62.5kg × 7. Without a record, that calculation is impossible and every week starts over.
    </Callout>
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Your goal changes the rep range, not the exercise list

"Get stronger", "get bigger", "build endurance" — the bench press is still the bench press in all three. What actually changes is **how many reps you do**, and set count and rest interval follow from that.

| Goal | Rep range | Sets | Rest between sets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strength | 3–6 | 3–5 | 2–3 min |
| Muscle size | 6–12 | 3–4 | 60–90 sec |
| Endurance | 12–20 | 2–3 | 30–60 sec |

These are widely used reference ranges, not hard rules. In practice they overlap, and mixing within a session — 6–8 reps on the compound, 12–15 on the accessories — is more common than picking one row.

If you're new, **start in the 8–12 range** regardless of goal. It's light enough to learn the movement and repetitive enough that the pattern sticks.

<Callout type="note" title="Rest intervals belong in the plan too">
  Rest between sets is the hidden variable that sets session length. Four exercises at three sets with two minutes of rest is more than twenty minutes of standing still. If you've decided on four exercises in a 60-minute session, rest has to sit inside 60–90 seconds for the planned volume to fit.
</Callout>

## What to drop first on a short week

Plans usually break at the moment you realize you only have forty minutes. Cancelling the whole session is the most expensive response. With a subtraction order decided in advance, even a 30-minute session stays part of the plan.

**Subtract in this order.**

1. **Accessories first** — remove one or two single-joint movements. The compound lifts already cover the same area, so this loses the least
2. **Then sets** — take the remaining exercises from three sets to two. Keeping the movement frequency beats deleting the movement
3. **Compounds stay until last** — hold on to one or two of bench press, lat pulldown, or a squat pattern. If those lose their log entry, next week's load has nothing to be calculated from
4. **Still not enough? A 20-minute session** — one compound for three sets, logged. Keeping attendance and the record unbroken has value on its own

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't stack missed sessions onto next week">
  Doing one of three planned sessions and then scheduling five for next week usually breaks that week too. Keep next week at three, and only lower the target to two after two consecutive half-weeks. Lowering a target isn't failure — it's the adjustment that keeps the next four weeks alive.
</Callout>

## Week two takes three minutes, not fifteen

From the second week, Steps 1–3 don't repeat. Session count, session length, and split stay put, so **the only things that move are load and reps.**

- Hit every target rep last week → add 2.5–5kg
- Fell short on the last set → hold the same load one more week
- Something hurt → swap that exercise or cut its volume

That's the point where planning stops being a Sunday-night task.

## Every four weeks, audit the plan itself

Week to week only the loads move, but every fourth week is worth spending on the frame. Three things to look at.

- **The gap between planned and actual sessions** — if you came up one short every week for four weeks, the target number is wrong, not your discipline. Set the next four weeks' baseline to the actual number
- **Any lift that never moved** — it probably kept sliding to the end of the session, or started too heavy, or got done fatigued every time. Move it to the front of the session and watch it for another four weeks
- **Whether sessions run long** — a 60-minute plan that always takes 80 is usually rest creep, not too many exercises. A timer brings it back inside the plan without removing anything

## Wrap-up

The reason most weeks slip isn't time — it's the weight of deciding.

Lock two numbers and most of the remaining decisions follow. One sheet of paper is enough for this week.
