6 AM at the Han River park, a group in matching shirts knocks out a 5K together.

Running crews have visibly multiplied among Korean office workers over the last 1–2 years. Accountability, pace partners, post-run beers — all the social glue that solo training lacks.

But there are gaps a crew structurally can't fill.

## What running crews do well — and don't

Running crews solve a **people** problem. Anything that depends on your individual body doesn't get solved inside the group.

What crews are good at:

- You feel guilty bailing on the group → attendance up
- Someone next to you holding the same pace gets you through the wall
- Post-run meal/drinks build a community

<Callout type="note" title="What running crews don't cover">
- **Personal recovery** — only you know the early signs of injury
- **Strength training** — without glute/core work, miles add up to injury
- **Progressive overload** — same pace forever leads to plateaus
- **Off-day planning** — rainy days, intervals, rest days are on you
</Callout>

## Why miles without strength = injury

Running puts **2–3× body weight on a single leg, repeatedly.** When muscle can't absorb that impact, the remaining load goes into the joint.

Common running injuries — runner's knee, shin splints, achilles problems — frequently start at that point. If the glutes, core, and hamstrings don't carry their share, the knee and ankle take more of it at the same mileage.

Focus only on distance and pace, and you'll lose a full month at some point.

The fix itself is simple: **1–2 strength sessions per week, 30–40 minutes.** The friction is deciding *what* to do at *what* intensity, every week.

## How to build a runner's strength session

Priority goes to **the tissue that absorbs landing impact.** Fill one session with these three slots and you have a complete workout.

| Slot | Purpose | Example movements |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Lower compound | Absorb landing impact | Squat variations, Romanian deadlift, lunges |
| 2. Core | Limit torso sway | Plank, side plank, dead bug |
| 3. Calf / ankle | Push-off and stability | Calf raise, single-leg balance |

Three sets per movement, starting at a load you can move for 10 clean reps. A runner's goal isn't maximal strength but tolerance for repeated impact, so holding position matters more than moving heavy.

<Callout type="warning" title="Placement decides recovery">
  Putting leg-focused strength work the day before a long run means form breaks down first the next morning. Keep it off interval days too. If crew runs are Mon/Wed/Fri, then Tue/Thu strength, Saturday long run, Sunday rest is a workable layout.
</Callout>

## A sample week

Mixing crew runs with strength work produces roughly this shape. Treat it as a starting point and adjust to your own recovery.

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon · Wed · Fri | Crew run (or solo) 5–10K |
| Tue | Strength A — lower compound + core (40 min) |
| Thu | Strength B — upper + core + ankle (40 min) |
| Sat | Long run (LSD) or full rest |
| Sun | Full rest + plan check for next week |

If five days is too much, cutting strength to Tuesday only beats dropping it entirely.

PocketFit isn't aimed at your running — it's aimed at **every decision outside the run**, which is exactly the space a crew leaves open.

## Wrap-up — "Together you go far, alone you handle the details"

Running crews solve the **people** problem. Strength planning solves the **decision** problem.

The same person makes both kinds of decisions every week. Neither one alone tends to last past a year.
