When you write your first resume, the question that hits you hardest isn't "what do I fill in?"

It's "how do I know if this is any good?"

There's no reference point — no one to compare against, no clear standard. So you fill a section, second-guess it, rewrite it, and end up going in circles.

This guide breaks that problem into 30 concrete checkpoints you can work through on your own, from basic contact information to job posting keyword alignment.

## Why the absence of a "good resume" benchmark stops first-timers cold

The biggest trap in writing your first resume isn't the blank page — it's **not knowing when you're done**.

Someone who has been through several application cycles develops an instinct: they can feel when a bullet point is weak. That instinct is just accumulated feedback. First-timers don't have it yet, so they tend to judge completeness by whether the fields are filled in rather than whether the content is strong. The result is a resume that looks complete but reads as vague.

The second trap is **not knowing how much role-specificity matters**. A software engineer's resume and a marketing coordinator's resume emphasize entirely different things — and even within the same role, each job posting asks for its own mix of skills and experience. Generic "how to write a good resume" content almost never covers that gap.

<Callout type="note" title="How to use this checklist">
  Read each item and check it against your own resume. Anything unchecked is a revision point.
  Don't fix everything at once — change one or two items, then run the same checklist again.
</Callout>

## The 30-item checklist

### A. Contact information

- [ ] Your full name appears clearly at the very top of the first page
- [ ] Your phone number and email address are current and actively checked
- [ ] Your email address looks professional (avoid handles like nickname123@... for job applications)
- [ ] Any links you've included — LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio — point to pages that are up to date
- [ ] You've removed personal information that isn't relevant to the role (e.g., date of birth, photo — confirm whether the target employer requires these before including)

### B. Formatting and readability

- [ ] The resume fits on **one page** (one to two pages maximum; one page is the standard for entry-level and internship candidates)
- [ ] You're using no more than two fonts, at a consistent size (10–11 pt for body text is a common baseline)
- [ ] Margins give the page enough breathing room to read comfortably
- [ ] The layout holds together when you export to PDF — no shifted columns or broken lines
- [ ] You've run a spell-check pass (and ideally a grammar check) before considering it final

### C. Summary or objective statement

- [ ] There's a 2–3 line summary near the top (optional, but a well-written one makes a strong first impression)
- [ ] The summary describes a **specific role or contribution** rather than a trait ("passionate team player who…" adds little signal)
- [ ] The summary naturally includes one or two keywords from the roles you're targeting

### D. Experience (projects, internships, part-time work, extracurriculars)

- [ ] Every entry shows **dates, organization name, and your title or role**
- [ ] Bullets are written as "did X and achieved Y" rather than "was responsible for X" — outcome-first framing lands better
- [ ] At least some results include numbers (e.g., "reduced page load time by 30%", "grew Instagram following from 500 to 1,200")
- [ ] For team projects, your individual contribution is explicit ("built and shipped the backend API" rather than "developed the app as a team")
- [ ] Entries are in reverse chronological order (most recent first)

### E. Skills

- [ ] The skills section only lists things that are actually relevant to the roles you're applying for
- [ ] Proficiency claims are honest — "familiar with" or "working knowledge of" is clearer than blanket "proficient in" for tools you've touched briefly
- [ ] Languages, frameworks, and tools are organized into readable categories rather than a flat list
- [ ] You could answer a follow-up interview question about every item listed — nothing is on there just to fill space

### F. Education, certifications, and training

- [ ] Institution name, field of study, and graduation year (or expected graduation year) are present
- [ ] Relevant certifications or completed courses are listed and tied to the type of roles you're targeting
- [ ] Certificates that have no connection to the role have been removed or deprioritized to save space

### G. Job posting keyword alignment

- [ ] You've read the job posting for each role you plan to apply to and pulled out **5–10 core keywords**
- [ ] Each of those keywords appears somewhere in your resume — as an exact term or a close equivalent
- [ ] You've checked the "required qualifications" section of the posting and confirmed your resume speaks to each one

### H. Final pre-submission checks

- [ ] You've asked someone to skim the resume for 30 seconds and then describe what kind of candidate you are — their answer should match what you intended
- [ ] The file is named something like **FirstName_LastName_Role_Resume.pdf** rather than "resume.pdf" or "final_v3.pdf"
- [ ] You've opened the actual file on your device and confirmed it displays correctly

<Callout type="tip" title="How did you score?">
  25 or more checked: run the three blind-spot checks below, then submit.
  15–24 checked: focus on sections D and G first (experience framing and posting keyword matching) — those move the needle most.
  Fewer than 15: content revision comes before formatting. Go back through your experience bullets and rewrite each one with a concrete result.
</Callout>

## After the checklist — three ways to find your own blind spots

**Self-review has a hard limit: you get used to your own sentences and stop seeing what's odd about them.** Three methods surface those blind spots without any tooling.

### 1. The 30-second impression test

Give the resume to one person — a friend, a family member — for **30 seconds only**, then take it away and ask: "what kind of candidate is this?"

If the answer doesn't match what you intended, the content isn't lacking; **the important items are sitting too low on the page.** Reordering the top summary and the first experience entry usually fixes it.

### 2. Read it out loud

Read the resume start to finish out loud. Sentences your eye skips over will trip your tongue.

A sentence that leaves you out of breath is too long. A sentence that makes you cringe as you say it is one you've overstated without evidence. Both kinds cause trouble under interview follow-up, so mark them.

### 3. Lay it next to the posting, line by line

Put the posting on the left and your resume on the right, and **for every requirement line, find and mark the resume sentence that answers it.**

If two or more lines have no match, either the posting is a stretch right now, or you did the work and never wrote it down. The second case is far more common.

<Callout type="important" title="If you use an AI tool">
  AI resume tools are worth using for structure, redundancy, and vague phrasing, but they can't judge how hard the work actually was. Treat the output as **reference-level feedback**, and don't follow "make this more impactful" into exaggeration — that sentence becomes an interview question later.
</Callout>

## Wrap-up

The hardest part of writing your first resume isn't putting words on the page — it's **not knowing what "done" looks like**.

The 30 checkpoints above give you a benchmark you can apply on your own. Once you've worked through the list, run the 30-second test, the read-aloud pass, and the line-by-line posting comparison, then fix one or two unchecked items at a time and run the list again.

Work through it one section at a time.
