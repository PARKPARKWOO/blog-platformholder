When you write your first resume, the question that hits you hardest isn't "what do I fill in?"

It's "how do I know if this is any good?"

You may not yet have a repeatable reference point. That can lead to filling a section, second-guessing it, and rewriting without a clear review question.

This guide breaks that problem into 30 concrete checkpoints you can work through on your own, from basic contact information to job posting keyword alignment.

## Use the checklist as an editing reference

A first resume can be difficult to assess without a repeatable review method. A checklist makes individual questions visible, but it does not establish whether an employer will select the application.

Role-specificity also varies. A software-engineering posting and a marketing-coordinator posting may ask for different evidence, and postings with the same title can still differ. Use the current employer's published instructions and duties as the boundary.

<Callout type="note" title="How to use this checklist">
  Read each item and check it against your own resume. Anything unchecked is a revision point.
  Don't fix everything at once — change one or two items, then run the same checklist again.
</Callout>

## The 30-item checklist

### A. Contact information

- [ ] Your full name appears clearly at the very top of the first page
- [ ] Your phone number and email address are current and actively checked
- [ ] Your email follows the employer's instructions and is easy to identify as a contact address
- [ ] Any links you've included — LinkedIn, GitHub, portfolio — point to pages that are up to date
- [ ] You've removed personal information that isn't relevant to the role (e.g., date of birth, photo — confirm whether the target employer requires these before including)

### B. Formatting and readability

- [ ] You checked the employer's length and file-format instructions (if none exist, use one to two pages as an entry-level starting point)
- [ ] Type and sizing are consistent and readable in the submitted file
- [ ] Margins give the page enough breathing room to read comfortably
- [ ] The layout holds together when you export to PDF — no shifted columns or broken lines
- [ ] You've run a spell-check pass (and ideally a grammar check) before considering it final

### C. Summary or objective statement

- [ ] If you include a summary, it states the role and evidence compactly (omit it when the employer's format calls for something else)
- [ ] The summary describes a **specific role or contribution** rather than a trait ("passionate team player who…" adds little signal)
- [ ] The summary naturally includes one or two keywords from the roles you're targeting

### D. Experience (projects, internships, part-time work, extracurriculars)

- [ ] Every entry shows **dates, organization name, and your title or role**
- [ ] Bullets identify what you did and include a result only when you have evidence for it
- [ ] Results include numbers only where you have measurement evidence (examples are fictional and should be backed by a method)
- [ ] For team projects, your individual contribution is explicit ("built and shipped the backend API" rather than "developed the app as a team")
- [ ] If the employer gives no ordering instruction, you considered reverse chronological order as a starting point

### E. Skills

- [ ] The skills section only lists things that are actually relevant to the roles you're applying for
- [ ] Proficiency claims are honest — "familiar with" or "working knowledge of" is clearer than blanket "proficient in" for tools you've touched briefly
- [ ] Languages, frameworks, and tools are organized into readable categories rather than a flat list
- [ ] You could answer a follow-up interview question about every item listed — nothing is on there just to fill space

### F. Education, certifications, and training

- [ ] Institution name, field of study, and graduation year (or expected graduation year) are present
- [ ] Relevant certifications or completed courses are listed and tied to the type of roles you're targeting
- [ ] Certificates that have no connection to the role have been removed or deprioritized to save space

### G. Job posting keyword alignment

- [ ] You've read the job posting and selected the duties and qualifications relevant to your evidence
- [ ] Each of those keywords appears somewhere in your resume — as an exact term or a close equivalent
- [ ] You've checked the "required qualifications" section of the posting and confirmed your resume speaks to each one

### H. Final pre-submission checks

- [ ] If useful, you've asked someone to skim each version for the same short interval and recorded what they noticed first
- [ ] The file is named something like **FirstName_LastName_Role_Resume.pdf** rather than "resume.pdf" or "final_v3.pdf"
- [ ] You've opened the actual file on your device and confirmed it displays correctly

<Callout type="tip" title="Do not turn the check count into a score">
  The total does not diagnose resume quality or predict an outcome. Group unchecked items into employer requirements, evidence accuracy, and readability, then choose the next revision based on the current posting.
</Callout>

## After the checklist — three ways to find your own blind spots

Self-review can be supplemented with other editing checks. The three methods below produce additional observations; none is a diagnosis or hiring test.

### 1. The 30-second impression test

Give the resume to one person — a friend or family member — for **30 seconds only**, then take it away and ask: "what did you notice first?" Thirty seconds is a controlled comparison interval for your own versions, not a claim about how long recruiters read.

If the answer doesn't match what you intended, inspect **both placement and evidence**. Reorder the top summary and first experience entry, run the same test again, and clarify the bullets if the result is still vague.

### 2. Read it out loud

Read the resume start to finish out loud. Mark any sentence that feels difficult to parse, then check its length, structure, and evidence. A spoken pass is one editing method, not proof that a sentence is good or bad.

### 3. Lay it next to the posting, line by line

Put the posting on the left and your resume on the right, and **for every requirement line, find and mark the resume sentence that answers it.**

For each line with no match, mark which case applies: you did the work but omitted it, you have transferable evidence that needs an honest explanation, or you do not yet have evidence. Revise only with facts you can support.

<Callout type="important" title="If you use an AI tool">
  Treat any AI output as a suggestion to verify. Check it against the posting, your records, privacy terms, and the facts of your experience. Reject wording that exaggerates your role or invents a result.
</Callout>

## The formula for turning a bullet into a result

If a responsibility-only bullet needs more context, the prompts below offer one way to inspect it.

Split the sentence into four pieces and the rewrite becomes mechanical.

> **[situation] + [what you did] + [what changed] + [how much]**

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Handled department promotion | Ran the department Instagram three times a week, growing followers from 500 to 1,200 over six months |
| Café part-time job | Rearranged the order-handoff flow, cutting average peak-hour wait time |
| Participated in development on a team project | Designed and built the signup and auth API as the sole backend owner on a four-person team |
| Ran a data analysis study group | Ran a 12-week study group, prepared weekly session material, kept 8 of 10 members active |
| Various volunteer work | 14 weekend shifts (56 hours) at a local library, assisting the children's reading program |

The right column isn't a more impressive experience. It's **the same experience, written in pieces**.

### When no number exists — three substitutes

Not every experience has a measured outcome. The following fields can add factual context when they are relevant and documented.

1. **Frequency and duration** — "three times a week", "over six months", "14 sessions". This describes activity, not an outcome.
2. **Scope and scale** — "a team of four", "a 200-member community", "across three departments". It shows the **size** of the work.
3. **Before and after** — "moved attendance tracking from paper to a shared spreadsheet." No number, but the **direction of the change** survives.

If none of the three applies, review the entry against role relevance, employer instructions, and duplication before deciding whether to keep it.

### Two things to avoid here

- **Presenting an estimate as a measurement.** If you write "improved by 30%", keep the source and measurement method. If you cannot support the number, remove it rather than replace it with another unsupported effect claim.
- **Borrowing team results as your own.** "Team hit $2M in revenue" does not identify your contribution. State only the part you handled and label team-level context clearly.

## Applying to many places — what to change and what to freeze

Once you're applying broadly, the question becomes "do I rewrite this every time?" No. **Separate the layer that moves from the layer that doesn't** so you do not recreate factual bullets for every application.

**Stable factual layer**

- The body of every experience bullet — the work is a fact and doesn't change per company
- Education, certifications, contact details
- Supported results and their measurement notes

**Per-posting layer**

- Wording in a compact summary — use the posting's terms only where they describe your evidence accurately
- The **order** of experience entries — consider placing relevant evidence earlier when the employer's format allows it
- The order of the skills list — put the stack the posting names first
- The filename — `FirstName_LastName_Role_Resume.pdf`

This separation can reduce accidental changes to factual claims. Keep a one-sheet log with the date, company, and submitted filename.

## Wrap-up

One challenge in a first resume is deciding what to review before submission.

The 30 checkpoints above form an editing worksheet. Once you've worked through the list, optionally try the same-interval peer skim, the read-aloud pass, and the line-by-line posting comparison, then choose a manageable revision and run the list again.

Work through it one section at a time.
