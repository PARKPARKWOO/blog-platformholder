If your interview answers always sound the same, it isn't a willpower problem.

**You've been training on generic question lists.**

This guide turns a posting and resume into practice-question candidates. Eight to twelve prompts and 30 minutes are worksheet examples, not claims about the actual interview or the time every applicant needs.

## Why use the posting as the practice boundary

Actual question design varies by employer, interview stage, and interviewer, and applicants often cannot see that process. A safer approach is to use **the posting the employer chose to publish** as the boundary for your own practice.

The sequence below describes the applicant's worksheet, not the interviewer's internal process:

1. Read your resume
2. Mark which lines of the target posting overlap with it
3. Turn overlapping lines into practice questions about the depth of your evidence
4. Turn non-overlapping lines into practice questions about how you would explain the gap

Steps 3 and 4 separate areas where you already have evidence from areas that need an honest explanation. The amount of time saved will vary.

<Callout type="important" title="Why '100 common questions' underperforms">
  Generic lists can help with broad practice, but they do not connect a specific posting to your evidence. This worksheet adds that missing comparison without claiming to know what the interviewer will ask.
</Callout>

## The five-step worksheet

<Steps>
  <Step title="Step 1 — Copy 5–10 requirement lines verbatim">
    **Copy, don't summarize.** The moment you paraphrase, the posting's own vocabulary disappears — and that vocabulary is the raw material for the questions.

    Open a document and paste 5–10 lines from the posting's "requirements" and "responsibilities" into the left column, word for word.

    - Include "nice to have" lines when they connect to real experience
    - Keep a broad line such as "strong communication skills" only if you can attach a specific example
    - Review tool or technology lines as either evidence or an honest gap

    Time: 5 minutes
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 2 — Attach one line of resume evidence to each requirement">
    In the right column, paste **a sentence that already exists in your resume**. Don't invent anything.

    | Posting requirement (verbatim) | My resume evidence (verbatim) |
    |---|---|
    | "Experience designing APIs under high traffic" | "(fictional example) Designed and operated a payments API at ~3M requests/day" |
    | "Experience with Kafka-based event processing" | *(blank)* |
    | "Comfortable in a strong testing culture" | "Introduced integration tests; deploy rollbacks decreased" |

    If you can't find evidence, **leave it blank.** A blank isn't embarrassing — a blank becomes a question in step 3.

    <Callout type="tip">
      If you actually did the work but there's no sentence for it in your resume, that's a resume problem, not an interview problem. Add the line to your resume first.
    </Callout>

    Time: 10 minutes
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 3 — Filled rows become depth questions, blanks become gap questions">
    Convert each row into a question. There are only two rules.

    **Right column filled → depth question**
    - "What was the hardest trade-off when you designed that API?"
    - "At what point did 3M requests/day start to bottleneck, and what did you change?"
    - "If you did it again, which decision would you make differently?"

    **Right column blank → gap question**
    - "You haven't worked with Kafka — how would you pick it up after joining?"
    - "Have you solved a similar problem with a different tool?"

    Write one prompt per requirement and add a follow-up where useful. That produces **roughly 8–12 practice prompts** in this worksheet. The number is a scoping choice, not a claim about the interview.

    Time: 10 minutes
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 4 — Draft a 60-second answer skeleton per question">
    Don't write full sentences. Write **four lines**. If you memorize prose, one missing word collapses the whole answer.

    1. **Situation** — when, under what constraints
    2. **Action** — one decision you actually made
    3. **Result** — with a number where possible
    4. **Link** — which line of *this* posting it maps to

    The fourth line is the one that matters. With it, the same experience produces a different answer at every company.

    For gap questions, two lines are enough: **acknowledge + plan**. "I haven't done that yet" followed by "I've handled a similar problem with ○○, and I plan to get through ○○ before I start."

    Time: 2–3 minutes per question
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 5 — Say it out loud, then read the transcript">
    Silent reading and speaking aloud test different things. Transcribing a spoken answer makes repeated wording available to mark directly.

    Cover the skeleton, answer out loud, then transcribe what you said and read it back. Check three things:

    - Did it finish inside the example 60-second target? (If not, review the balance among situation, action, result, and link.)
    - **How many sentences still make sense with the company name deleted?** If many do, line 4 (the link) is missing.
    - How many times did "passionate", "growth", or "various" appear?

    You can transcribe by hand or with a speech-to-text tool. What matters is getting **your spoken words into visible text**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## When the posting is three vague lines — four places to reconstruct requirements

The whole method assumes **the posting actually lists requirements**. Plenty of postings don't: three lines of "relevant experience" and "someone to grow with our team," and that's it.

In that case you rebuild the requirements from outside the posting. There are four sources.

**1. The company's official engineering blog or newsroom**
Recent first-party posts show projects and terms the company has chosen to publish. They may describe a different team, so mark any prompt from them `(verify)`.

**2. Other postings at the same company**
Companies reuse posting templates. A different role's posting frequently spells out the shared context — how the team collaborates, which tools they use, how the org is structured — in far more detail.

**3. Public postings for similar roles**
Other employers' postings can reveal a topic you missed, but they do not establish this employer's requirements. Mark them `(industry reference)`.

**4. Official team pages**
If the company publishes team roles or technologies, use them to form a verification question. Do not infer hiring requirements from an individual's profile.

<Callout type="warning" title="Mark reconstructed requirements as assumptions">
  Anything pulled from outside the posting is not a confirmed requirement. Tag it `(assumed)` in your table and raise it in the interview **as a question** — "is that something this team handles as well?" State it as fact and, if it isn't their work, you look less prepared rather than more.
</Callout>

## Reviewing your 8–12 questions the night before

If you built the question set and then re-read it cold the night before, you run out of time. **Rank it in advance** and 30 minutes is enough.

Order by three criteria.

1. **Start with duties repeated or explained concretely.** Order alone does not prove priority.
2. **Gap questions before depth questions.** They need more preparation because you do not yet have direct evidence, not because this predicts the hiring outcome.
3. **Number-carrying answers last.** Figures are the easiest thing to forget, so check them closest to the interview.

A 30-minute split that works:

| Time | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0–10 min | Say the "acknowledge + plan" two-liners for your 2–3 gap questions out loud |
| 10–25 min | Skim the four-line skeletons of your top 4–5 depth questions, re-checking that each "link" line uses *this* posting's wording |
| 25–30 min | Re-read only the sentences that carry numbers, dates, or scale |

Review the rest if time remains. This is only a night-before allocation example; any employer-provided interview format or evaluation guidance comes first.

## Wrap-up

The hard part of interview prep is knowing **what to practice**.

The moment you copy 5–10 requirement lines verbatim and attach your resume evidence, the practice list appears in front of you. It's something you can do today, with no tool at all.
