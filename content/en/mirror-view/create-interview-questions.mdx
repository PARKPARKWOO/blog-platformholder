If your interview answers sound similar across roles, the practice material may be one factor.

This guide turns a posting and resume into practice-question candidates without claiming to know the employer's actual questions or the time you need.

## Why use the posting as the practice boundary

Actual question design varies by employer, interview stage, and interviewer, and applicants often cannot see that process. A safer approach is to use **the posting the employer chose to publish** as the boundary for your own practice.

The sequence below describes the applicant's worksheet, not the interviewer's internal process:

1. Read your resume
2. Mark which lines of the target posting overlap with it
3. Turn overlapping lines into practice questions about the depth of your evidence
4. Turn non-overlapping lines into practice questions about how you would explain the gap

Steps 3 and 4 separate areas where you already have evidence from areas that need an honest explanation. The amount of time saved will vary.

<Callout type="important" title="What this worksheet adds to a general question list">
  Generic lists can help with broad practice, but they do not connect a specific posting to your evidence. This worksheet adds that missing comparison without claiming to know what the interviewer will ask.
</Callout>

## The five-step worksheet

<Steps>
  <Step title="Step 1 — Copy relevant requirement lines verbatim">
    **Copy, don't summarize.** The moment you paraphrase, the posting's own vocabulary disappears — and that vocabulary is the raw material for the questions.

    Open a document and paste the lines you want to practice from the posting's "requirements" and "responsibilities" into the left column.

    - Include "nice to have" lines when they connect to real experience
    - Keep a broad line such as "strong communication skills" only if you can attach a specific example
    - Review tool or technology lines as either evidence or an honest gap

  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 2 — Attach one line of resume evidence to each requirement">
    In the right column, paste **a sentence that already exists in your resume**. Don't invent anything.

    | Posting requirement (verbatim) | My resume evidence (verbatim) |
    |---|---|
    | "Experience designing APIs under high traffic" | "(fictional example) Designed and operated a payments API at ~3M requests/day" |
    | "Experience with Kafka-based event processing" | *(blank)* |
    | "Comfortable in a strong testing culture" | "Introduced integration tests; deploy rollbacks decreased" |

    If you can't find evidence, **leave it blank.** A blank isn't embarrassing — a blank becomes a question in step 3.

    <Callout type="tip">
      If you did the work but cannot find it in your resume, consider whether the resume is missing relevant evidence. Add it only when it is truthful, relevant, and allowed by the employer's format.
    </Callout>

  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 3 — Filled rows become depth questions, blanks become gap questions">
    Convert each row into a question using these two worksheet patterns.

    **Right column filled → depth question**
    - "What was the hardest trade-off when you designed that API?"
    - "At what point did 3M requests/day start to bottleneck, and what did you change?"
    - "If you did it again, which decision would you make differently?"

    **Right column blank → gap question**
    - "You haven't worked with Kafka — how would you pick it up after joining?"
    - "Have you solved a similar problem with a different tool?"

    Write one prompt per selected requirement and add a follow-up when it helps you inspect your evidence. Choose a manageable subset rather than treating any count as an interview formula.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 4 — Draft a compact answer outline per question">
    One optional outline uses **four fields** rather than full prose:

    1. **Situation** — when, under what constraints
    2. **Action** — one decision you actually made
    3. **Result** — with a number where possible
    4. **Link** — which line of *this* posting it maps to

    The fourth field records why you selected this evidence for the current posting. It does not need to make every sentence company-specific.

    For a gap question, you can try **acknowledge + related evidence or plan**. Keep the plan realistic and subordinate to any employer guidance.
  </Step>

  <Step title="Step 5 — Say it out loud, then read the transcript">
    Silent reading and speaking aloud test different things. Transcribing a spoken answer makes repeated wording available to mark directly.

    Cover the skeleton, answer out loud, then transcribe what you said and read it back. Check three things:

    - Did it follow the employer's format, if one was provided? Otherwise, is the balance among situation, action, supported result, and link useful for your own comparison?
    - **How many sentences still make sense with the company name deleted?** If many do, line 4 (the link) is missing.
    - How many times did "passionate", "growth", or "various" appear?

    You can transcribe by hand or with a speech-to-text tool. What matters is getting **your spoken words into visible text**.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## When the posting is brief — first-party places to form hypotheses

This worksheet needs concrete duties or qualifications from the posting. A brief posting may not provide enough evidence for detailed prompts.

Do not rebuild requirements from outside material. You can use the following employer-controlled sources to write questions that remain explicitly unverified.

**1. The company's official engineering blog or newsroom**
Recent first-party posts show projects and terms the company has chosen to publish. They may describe a different team, so mark any prompt from them `(verify)`.

**2. Other postings from the same employer**
A different role or team may publish additional context. Treat it as evidence only for that posting and use it solely to form a question about the target opening.

**3. Official team pages**
If the company publishes team roles or technologies, use them to form a verification question. Do not infer hiring requirements from an individual's profile.

<Callout type="warning" title="Label anything outside the posting as a hypothesis">
  Material from another official employer page is not a confirmed requirement for this opening. Tag it `(hypothesis)` and keep it as a verification question. Competitor postings and individual employee profiles are not evidence of this employer's requirements.
</Callout>

## Reviewing the practice set before the interview

If you want to prioritize the set, the following is one optional editorial order.

1. **Start with duties repeated or explained concretely.** Order alone does not prove priority.
2. **Rows without direct evidence.** Decide whether you have truthful related evidence, a realistic plan, or simply need to acknowledge the gap.
3. **Answers containing figures, dates, or scale.** Re-check each value against your records and measurement notes.

Use as much or as little time as your schedule allows. Any employer-provided interview format or evaluation guidance comes first.

## Wrap-up

One interview-preparation question is **what to practice**.

Copy selected requirement lines and attach resume evidence to create a bounded practice list. The list remains a worksheet, not a prediction.
