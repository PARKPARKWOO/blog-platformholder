Plenty of people get one round of resume feedback, make the edits, and move on.

But most of the value shows up **from the second round onward.** The first pass gets spent clearing the obvious surface problems; structural issues only become visible once those are gone.

Look at the same resume from five different angles and each angle returns something different. Here are the five angles.

## Why one pass isn't enough

**Resume feedback output depends entirely on two inputs.**

1. **Which posting you're holding it against** — the same resume flips strengths and weaknesses depending on the role
2. **What you're asking** — "does the structure read?" / "does this fit the role?" / "what would an interviewer probe?" are three different questions

One round of feedback is one combination of those. To see the resume in 3D, you have to change the angle and look again.

<Callout type="important" title="Feedback is a conversation, not a diagnosis">
It isn't a one-shot diagnostic. It pays off as a cycle: **get feedback → fix → check again.** The five items below assume you run through them once each, in order.
</Callout>

## The five angles

<Steps>
  <Step title="1. First pass: the resume alone, no posting">
    **Matching comes later. Get the resume's own baseline first.**

    What you're looking for here are the posting-independent problems:

    - **Structure**: is the section order built for the reader, and are the proportions right?
    - **Phrasing**: how many empty modifiers — "various", "several", "many" — are in there?
    - **Redundancy**: does the same experience appear in the summary, the work history, *and* the projects?
    - **Omissions**: is the best thing you did actually written down anywhere?

    With a posting in hand, these get misread as "not a fit for this company." That's why the order matters.

    Time: 15–20 minutes
  </Step>

  <Step title="2. Hold the same resume against 3–5 postings and read the variance">
    **With a single posting you can't tell whether a finding is a resume problem or a fit problem.**

    Pick 3–5 postings you care about, pull out each one's requirement lines, and build a table by checking the same resume against each.

    | Requirement | Posting A | Posting B | Posting C |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | High-traffic systems | evidence | not required | evidence |
    | Domain modeling | empty | empty | empty |
    | Testing culture | evidence | evidence | not required |

    Reading it is simple.

    - **Empty across several postings** → a real resume weakness (domain modeling)
    - **Empty in one posting only** → a fit problem, solvable through targeting

    Picking the one item that's empty everywhere is the output of this step.
  </Step>

  <Step title="3. Fix 1–2 items, then re-check">
    Even if the pass flags 5–10 items, **don't fix them all at once.**

    Two reasons:

    - Too many edits and you can't tell which one worked
    - A rewritten line frequently creates a new problem

    The process:

    1. Pick the **1–2 highest-impact** items
    2. Edit only those
    3. **Re-check against the same criteria** and see whether the finding disappeared
    4. If it did, move to the next item

    <Callout type="tip">
      One cycle = "1–2 fixes + 1 re-check." Four to six cycles visibly changes a resume.
    </Callout>
  </Step>

  <Step title="4. Log the date, posting, and key finding for every cycle">
    **Without a log, you can be circling the same spot four weeks later and never notice.**

    Keep four lines per cycle:

    - Date
    - Target posting (if any)
    - The 1–2 key findings
    - The next change you plan to try

    Read the log in one sitting after 4–6 weeks and three things separate out:

    - Findings that stopped appearing → actually improved
    - **Findings that appear every single time** → the wording isn't the problem; the underlying experience is
    - Which type of posting you're consistently weak against → time to revisit targeting

    Time: 2 minutes per cycle
  </Step>

  <Step title="5. Carry the findings into your interview answers">
    **Resume feedback is half of interview prep**, because whatever stood out in the resume stands out to the interviewer too.

    Two directions:

    **Items flagged as "X is a strength"**
    → keywords to raise early in the interview
    → the evidence behind "what's your strength" and "why this company"

    **Items flagged as "Y is weak"**
    → the areas most likely to get probed
    → write it out in advance as **one line of acknowledgement + one line of plan**

    Once you've moved them over, the feedback list *is* a likely-question list. From here, continue into [the 5-step method for building questions from a posting and your resume](/en/blog/mirror-view/create-interview-questions).
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Three common mistakes

**1. Despairing at the first pass**

The number of findings in the first round reflects **how many surface problems haven't been cleared yet**, not the quality of your resume. Start worrying if the count doesn't drop in the second round.

**2. Over-polishing the phrasing**

Follow "make it more impactful" into exaggeration and the interview question "how exactly did you do this?" takes the whole thing down. **Fact-based clarification** is the ceiling.

**3. One pass and done**

The most common mistake. Four to six cycles is what creates the change.

## How to ask a person for a review — requests that get answered

Angles 2 and 3 go much faster with someone else's eyes on the page. The bottleneck is **the shape of the request.** "Could you take a look at my resume?" usually gets no reply — the reader doesn't know what to look for, and doing it properly would take half an hour.

Response rate isn't driven by how well you know the person. It's driven by **whether you kept their effort under ten minutes.**

### Four things to put in the message

1. **The target role, one line** — "applying to backend roles, mostly commerce domain"
2. **One or two specific questions** — "of these three experience entries, which one reads weakest for this role?"
3. **The reply format you'll accept** — "one line is plenty" / "just the number is fine"
4. **A deadline that isn't one** — "no rush if this week doesn't work"

<Callout type="tip" title="Same ask, different outcome">
  ❌ "Whenever you have time, could you take a look at my resume? Attached."

  ✅ "I'm applying to backend roles — in the attached resume, could you pick **which one of the three experience entries reads weakest for this kind of role**? Just the number is fine."

  The second one can be answered in 30 seconds.
</Callout>

### Different reviewers, different questions

- **Someone doing the same job** — "does this phrasing land in this field?", "how hard does this project read?"
- **Someone who has hired** — "what gets read first?", "if you passed on this, which line caused it?"
- **A friend outside the field** — "after 30 seconds, what kind of person is this?" (non-specialists are actually more accurate here)
- **An AI tool** — form-level problems: structure, redundancy, vague modifiers

Don't ask one person all four. The answers blur together.

## Three signals that feedback shouldn't be applied as given

Once you're collecting feedback, contradictory advice piles up. Apply all of it and the resume stops belonging to anyone. Decide the **filters** in advance.

**1. Suggestions that change the underlying facts**
"You basically led that — why not just say you led it?" This is where things break. The follow-up question "which parts did you lead?" arrives immediately. **Clarifying** the phrasing is the ceiling; changing the size of the fact crosses the line.

**2. Format notes from personal taste**
Fonts, colors, section order — different reviewers say opposite things. Apply it **only when two or more people raise the same note.** Follow one person's format preference and the next reviewer tells you to undo it.

**3. Advice given while misunderstanding the role**
"Trim the tech stack and lead with soft skills" flips depending on the job. That's exactly why the request message needs **one line naming the target role.**

<Callout type="important" title="How to settle contradictory feedback">
  When you can't tell which advice is right, let **the job posting** decide. If the posting uses that word, keep it; if it doesn't, cut it. The posting outranks the reviewer's taste.
</Callout>

## Wrap-up — "feedback is a conversation, not a tool"

Resume feedback isn't a **one-shot diagnostic** — it's a **repeated coaching cycle.**

The only thing you have to do today is item 1. Close the postings, open the resume, and count how many times the word "various" shows up. Start there.
