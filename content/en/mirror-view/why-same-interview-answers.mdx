Your resume gets through, but you keep slipping in interviews.

When the interviewer asks "why this company?" or "what's your strength?", your mind goes blank — and you end up giving **almost the same answer you gave at the last interview.**

It's not that you didn't prep. You read 100+ "common interview questions." So why does this happen?

## The limit of generic question lists — "common denominator only"

**Answers rehearsed on generic lists sound the same everywhere because those lists were designed to work everywhere.** That's not a flaw in your effort; it's what the material is.

The "100 interview questions" you find in books, blogs, and YouTube cover questions that work for **any role, any company, any candidate** — the **common denominator.**

The problem: real interviewers don't ask the common denominator. They usually think:

1. I read this candidate's resume
2. Which requirement lines in our posting match it?
3. Make a question that tests the depth of those matches
4. Probe how they'd close the gap on what doesn't match

= The interviewer's real question is **"Does this person fit *our* posting?"** That question isn't on any generic list, and it's different at every company.

<Callout type="important" title="Why the same answer keeps coming">
Answers practiced on generic questions are essentially **"answers that work for any company."** Change the company, the answer barely changes — and the interviewer reads that as "this person didn't really look at us."
</Callout>

## The four parts of a deep answer

**When an interviewer thinks "this candidate is sharp," the answer almost always contains all four of these.** Drop one and the impression changes noticeably.

### 1. Posting-specific context
- "Reading your recent ○○ announcement…"
- "The ○○ skill emphasized in this posting…"

### 2. Your concrete experience
- "Over the last year I worked on ○○…"
- "In that project I handled ○○ data…"

### 3. The link between the two
- "That experience maps directly to ○○ in this role…"

### 4. Honest acknowledgement of a gap + a plan
- "○○ is still my weak area, but I plan to close it by ○○ once I'm in…"

**The one most people drop is #3.** Parts 1 and 2 come naturally out of normal prep, but the sentence that joins them only appears when you put the resume and the posting side by side and do the matching. Without it, an answer describes a good experience and a good company, separately.

## Generic vs personalized — same question, different depth

**The whole difference comes down to one sentence.** Compare two answers to the same "why our company?":

**Generic (works for any company)**
> "You're a leader in your industry, growing fast, and your values align with mine. I want to grow in this environment."

**Personalized (this company + your resume matched)**
> "I read your recent ○○ launch announcement, and the ○○ work I did last quarter contributes directly to that direction. The ○○ point emphasized in the posting overlaps exactly with what I've done for six months."

The first answer survives having the company name deleted. The second leaves a hole the moment you delete it. **That gap is what reads as preparation.**

## The fastest way to check whether your answers repeat

**Say the answer out loud, then transcribe it and read it back.** Silent practice can't catch repetition.

Inside your head, sentences always feel smooth — the filler and the repeats get auto-corrected on the way through. A transcript has no auto-correction.

With the text in front of you, check three things:

1. **How many sentences still make sense with the company and product names deleted?** A lot of them means part #3 (the link) is missing.
2. **How many times did "passionate", "growth", "various", or "I learned a lot" appear?** Count them — it's usually more than you expected.
3. **Did the answer run past 60–90 seconds?** If so, the setup is usually long and the result and link are short.

You can transcribe by hand or with a speech-to-text tool. What matters is getting **your spoken words into visible text**.

## Three ways to get your spoken answer into text

There are three transcription methods, and **the goal is identical for all of them — making your own words visible as text.** Only the setup and the time differ.

**1. Writing it down by hand**
No setup required. But you can't write while you talk, so you speak once and then transcribe from memory — and memory quietly filters out the filler. That makes it **the least accurate method**. If your goal is catching repeated phrases, this one has a ceiling.

**2. A phone voice memo**
The recording survives, so you can replay it, and every "um", every "so yeah", every pause stays intact. The downside is that it isn't text. To count repeated phrases you still have to listen and write it out.

**3. Converting to text in the browser as you speak**

## Three patterns for writing the link sentence

Part #3 — the link — is the one most people drop. But "link them" isn't instruction enough to produce a sentence. **In practice you drop it into one of three patterns**, and all three start by borrowing one word from the posting.

### Pattern 1 — Quote: "the ○○ in your posting overlaps with the ○○ I handled"

The safest and most common. Quote the posting's wording, then name the point where your experience overlaps it.

> "The posting's line about 'designing APIs under high traffic' overlaps exactly with the payments API I ran at around 3M requests a day."

Change the company and the phrase you quote changes, so the answer changes with it automatically.

### Pattern 2 — Shared problem: "I've hit the problem that comes with that work"

Name a problem that **predictably follows** from the work described in the posting, and say you've been through it. This reads one level deeper than a quote.

> "The posting mentions launching a new service, and in that phase the schema keeps moving, so migrations pile up behind every deploy. I went through that stretch on my last project and settled it with ○○."

Don't assert what that team is currently experiencing, though. "It tends to happen" is a safer register than "you are dealing with."

### Pattern 3 — Next step: "so the first thing I'd look at is ○○"

Link the experience to the posting and carry it one move further. This works even with a short track record — often better for entry-level candidates.

> "That ○○ experience touches the ○○ in this role, so if I joined I'd want to spend the first stretch understanding how ○○ is currently structured."

### Rules that apply to all three

- Use **the posting's exact wording.** Paraphrase it and the "I read your posting" signal disappears
- **One link sentence per answer** is enough. Three of them reads as forced
- Only link **within work you've genuinely done.** Link something you haven't and it surfaces in the very next question

<Callout type="tip" title="How to check that a link sentence landed">
  Delete the company name and the posting's words from the sentence. **A hole should appear.** If the sentence still reads smoothly, it isn't a link yet — it's general description.
</Callout>

## What it actually costs to produce three company versions

The only burden here is time. But what you rewrite is **one link sentence**, so it's shorter than it sounds.

| Task | Time |
|---|---|
| Write experience cards (situation, action, result) — **company-independent, once** | 10 min per card |
| Copy 5–10 requirement lines — per company | 5 min |
| Write one link sentence — per question | 2 min per question |
| Say it out loud and review the transcript | 3 min per answer |

Build 8–10 experience cards up front and the only thing that grows with each new company is the bottom three rows. **If you're writing answers from scratch at every company, it's because the cards don't exist yet.**

The procedure for generating the questions themselves is written up as five steps in [how to build interview questions from a posting and your resume](/en/blog/mirror-view/create-interview-questions) — about 30 minutes per company.

## Closing

The hard part of prep isn't effort — it's knowing **what to practice.**

Practice that starts at the intersection of *your resume* and *this posting* — not a generic question list — produces a different kind of answer. And the only way to know whether it actually changed is to say it out loud and read it back as text.
